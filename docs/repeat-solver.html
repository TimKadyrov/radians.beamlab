Inside the Repeat Solver

The Repeat solver lists the altitudes near your target where a ground track truly repeats, and turns each one into the numbers a Case-2 filing declares. This page explains what the solver searches for, why only special altitudes qualify, and how to read every column of its results.

The question it answers

A repeating ground track is a promise: after some whole number of laps, the satellite retraces exactly the same lines over the Earth. The promise only holds at special altitudes — so the practical question is not “does my orbit repeat?” (at an arbitrary altitude it does not) but “which nearby altitudes repeat, and what does each cost me?” That is what the solver answers.

It matters only when you intend to make that promise. A free-drift filing (Case 1) and a declared-precession filing (Case 3) fly your target orbit exactly as entered — no solved repeat is needed, and you can skip the solver entirely. The solver exists for Case 2.

Everything rests on one number, explained in The Three Orbit Cases: each northbound equator crossing lands a fixed step S_pass west of the previous one. At 1200 km and 53° inclination, S_pass is 27.6653° per lap.

When a track closes

Stack the westward steps end to end around the equator. The track repeats after k laps precisely when those k steps add up to a whole number of full turns — m of them. One step short or long, and every later lap lands slightly offset: the residual is the drift you would fly with.

Only coprime pairs count. A pair like k = 26, m = 2 draws no new lines — it traces the 13-lap pattern twice — so the solver skips every pair with a common factor and reports each distinct pattern once.

Technical detail — the closure condition
repeat after k laps  ⇔  k · S_pass = m · 360°,  gcd(k, m) = 1
candidate m  = round(k · S_pass / 360)  for k = 1 … max orbits/cycle

S_pass comes from the same J2 secular rates the propagation uses (Rec. ITU-R S.1503-4 §D6.3.2, Steps 1–4).

Finding the exact altitude

At your target altitude the equality almost never holds — but S_pass grows steadily with altitude (a higher orbit takes longer per lap, so the Earth turns further underneath). That gives the solver a clean method: for each candidate pair it asks “at what altitude does S_pass equal exactly 360·m/k?” and closes in on the answer by repeated halving inside the search band you set. Candidates whose exact altitude falls outside the band are dropped.

Technical detail — the altitude solve
f(a) = S_pass(a) − 360 m / k   (monotone increasing in a)
bracket over [target − band, target + band]; 90 bisection halvings
candidates ranked by |exact altitude − target|, best 10 kept

Reading the results

Each row of the grid is one closed pattern, already converted into filing terms:

orbits k / days m — the pattern: k laps in m node-relative days. exact alt — where the pattern truly closes; delta is how far that is from your target. cycle — the repeat duration, already split as the filing’s rpt_prd_dd/hh/mm/ss. drift@target — the residual per cycle if you fly your target altitude instead of the exact one. spacing — the distance between adjacent tracks, 360/k. max keep_rnge — half that spacing: the widest longitude tolerance before neighbouring swept tracks collide (the tab refuses more).

The trade the table shows is general: short cycles (the one-day 13/1) sit closest to a round-number altitude and leave a generous tolerance, but space their tracks far apart; long cycles (the week-long grids) lay finer track meshes at the price of a tighter tolerance and a repeat period the station keeping must actually hold.

Checking a period of your own

The scan is not the only way in. If you already have a repeat in mind — from an existing filing, a mission plan, a published constellation — enter it directly as k orbits in m nodal days and it is validated like any candidate the scan found itself. A non-coprime pair is reduced first, because the shorter cycle is the true one: 26 orbits in 2 days is really the 13/1 pattern — after 13 laps the track has already closed, and the second time around adds nothing. The exact altitude that closes the reduced pair is then solved anywhere between 100 and 30 000 km, and the result appears as the highlighted top row of the grid: selectable like any other candidate, and flagged in red when its altitude falls outside the search band around your target — a warning that the repeat exists, just not near the orbit you meant to fly.

Proof by flight

The solver’s numbers are never left as algebra. When you select a row, the tab flies one full cycle at the exact altitude through the same propagation the EPFD calculation uses and draws the ground track: the filled dot is where it started, the ring where it ended. For a true repeat they sit on top of each other, and the printed closure angle says the same thing as a number — 0.0000° for the candidates above.
