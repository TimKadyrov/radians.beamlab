Inside the Repeat Solver

The Repeat solver finds orbits whose ground track, the path a satellite traces over the Earth, repeats: after k orbits in m days the satellite passes over exactly the same points again. It lists the repeats near your target orbit and gives each one the values a station-kept filing declares. Both station-kept cases take their repeat from here: Case 2, a repeating track held by station keeping, the small manoeuvres that keep a satellite on its planned path, and Case 3, which holds the track the same way and also supplies the rate at which the orbit’s plane turns. Free drift (Case 1) uses the target orbit as entered and does not need the solver.

What you set

The target orbit comes from the Start here sub-tab: altitude, inclination and eccentricity. The first line of 1 · Altitude mode repeats it. Shell: picks the shell of the document you are working on. Results update when you leave a field.

  • 1 · Altitude mode chooses the altitude a Case 2 shell files. adjusting altitude, the default, files the altitude where the track repeats by itself. fixed altitude files your target altitude, and the EPFD calculation then flies its drift every cycle. A Case 3 shell files your target altitude in either mode.
  • 2 · Declare a period of your own (fixed altitude only): Orbits (k) and Nodal days (m), the repeat you declare. It starts on the nearest repeat.
  • 2 · Repeat search (adjusting altitude only): Max orbits / cycle, the longest cycle searched, 120 by default, and Search band (km), how far above or below the target the altitude may move, 400 km by default.
  • 3 · Tolerance (keep_rnge): keep_rnge (deg), the longitude tolerance the filing promises either side of the track, 0.5° by default.

What you see:

  • The grid, top right: up to 10 repeats nearest your target, plus your declared pair when it is not among them. The selected row feeds the Station-keeping cases and Constellation sub-tabs and the saved design.
  • The status line below the settings: how many repeats were found and how far the nearest lies from the target. It reads no repeat inside the search band when there is none. It reads inputs out of range unless the altitude is 200 to 45 000 km, the inclination above 0 and below 180°, the eccentricity 0 or more and below 0.9, Max orbits / cycle 1 to 2000 and Search band (km) above 0.
  • The map, bottom right: one cycle of the selected row’s ground track. Its closure is printed under How the solver works.

When a track repeats

Watch the point where the satellite crosses the equator heading north. One orbit later it crosses a fixed step further west: the Earth has turned underneath, and the Earth’s equatorial bulge slowly turns the orbit’s plane. With the tab’s defaults, 1200 km, 53° and a circular orbit, this westward step is 27.6653° per orbit. The Three Orbit Cases explains it.

Lay the steps end to end along the equator. The track repeats after k orbits when those k steps add up to exactly m whole turns. If they miss by a little, each cycle starts slightly off from the last, and the track never repeats. The miss per cycle is the drift.

Only pairs with no common factor count. 26 orbits in 2 days traces the 13-orbit pattern twice and draws no new lines, so the solver skips it and lists each pattern once.

Tech · the repeat condition
repeat after k orbits  ⇔  k · S_pass = m · 360°,  gcd(k, m) = 1
m = round(k · S_pass / 360) at the target,  for k = 1 … Max orbits / cycle

S_pass is the westward step of the ascending node, the northbound equator crossing, per nodal orbit. It comes from the same J2 secular rates the propagation uses (Rec. ITU-R S.1503-4 §D6.3.2, Steps 1–4). m counts nodal days: turns of the Earth relative to the node. Each k is tried with this one m only.

Finding the exact altitude

At your target altitude the steps almost never add up exactly. But the westward step grows steadily with altitude: a higher satellite takes longer to go round, so the Earth turns further underneath. For each pair, the solver finds the one altitude where k steps make exactly m turns, by halving an interval inside the search band again and again. A pair whose altitude lies outside the band is dropped.

Tech · the altitude solve
f(a) = S_pass(a) − 360 · m / k   (increasing in a)
bracket [target − band, target + band]; 90 halvings
rows sorted by |exact alt − target|; the nearest 10 kept

When f does not change sign across the bracket, the pair’s altitude lies outside the band and the pair is dropped. Your own pair is solved over 100 to 30 000 km instead.

Reading the grid

Each row is one repeat, already in filing terms:

  • orbits k and days m: the track repeats after k orbits in m days.
  • exact alt (km): the altitude where the track repeats by itself. delta (km) is its distance from your target.
  • cycle@target: the repeat period at your own altitude, k of your orbits, in days and hh:mm:ss. A Case 2 shell in fixed altitude mode declares it.
  • cycle@exact: the repeat period at the exact altitude. A Case 2 shell in adjusting altitude mode, the default, declares it.
  • drift@target (deg/cyc): how far the track moves each cycle at your own altitude. It is the drift the EPFD calculation flies every cycle when the shell files that altitude, and the correction a real satellite’s station keeping would make. A negative drift falls short, east of the start; a positive one overshoots, west.
  • spacing (deg): the distance between neighbouring tracks at the equator.
  • max keep_rnge: half the spacing, the widest tolerance before the tolerance bands of neighbouring tracks overlap.

A light blue top row is the pair you declared. A red row has its exact altitude outside the search band; only your own pair can be red, since the search drops such pairs.

With the defaults, 1200 km, 53°, circular, Max orbits / cycle 120 and Search band (km) 400, the westward step is 27.6653° and one orbit takes 109.37 min. The first five rows, with 13/1 highlighted, the nearest repeat and the row the tab selects by default:

The rows show the usual trade. A short cycle, like the one-day 13/1, spaces its tracks far apart and allows a wide keep_rnge. A long cycle lays a finer mesh of tracks but allows only a narrow one. Tech · the columns
spacing = 360 / k     max keep_rnge = 180 / k
cycle = k · T_nodal, rounded to whole seconds → rpt_prd_dd/hh/mm/ss
drift@target = k · S_pass(target) − 360 · m

T_nodal is the time from one northbound equator crossing to the next: at the target altitude for cycle@target, at the exact altitude for cycle@exact.

Fixed or adjusting altitude

1 · Altitude mode decides the altitude a Case 2 shell files. It matters because the EPFD calculation flies the filed orbit as it is: the declared repeat period sets how long the run lasts, but nothing in the calculation holds the orbit on that repeat.

  • adjusting altitude, the default, adopts the selected row’s exact alt and declares cycle@exact. The filed orbit repeats by itself, so the track the calculation flies is the declared repeat. Selecting a row adopts its altitude.
  • fixed altitude keeps your target altitude and declares cycle@target as the repeat period. The track still drifts by drift@target each cycle, and the calculation flies that drift every cycle of the run. A real satellite would correct it by station keeping; the calculation does not. Selecting a row copies its pair into 2 · Declare a period of your own.

A Case 3 shell files your target altitude in both modes, with its own repeat period and by default the rate that closes the selected repeat at that altitude. The Three Orbit Cases explains it.

In fixed altitude mode the search settings are hidden but still apply: the grid and its red flag use the current Max orbits / cycle and Search band (km), 120 and 400 km by default.

Tech · what the shell declares, and how it is simulated

In both modes a Case 2 shell files f_stn_keep='Y', keep_rnge and rpt_prd_dd/hh/mm/ss (Rec. ITU-R S.1503-4 §B3.1); the Case 2 panel on the Station-keeping cases sub-tab shows them. The EPFD calculation, and the app’s own simulations, move a Case 2 orbit under the J2 rates at its filed altitude and sweep its northbound equator crossing evenly from keep_rnge west to keep_rnge east of its natural position over the run (§D6.3.6, eq (49)). The declared rpt_prd sets only the run length and keeps the time step from dividing it exactly (§D4.6.1). At the target altitude the simulated track therefore keeps its drift@target per cycle; at the exact altitude it has none. A Case 3 shell files the same fields at the target altitude, with rpt_prd = k · 2π/n₀ and f_precess='Y' with its rate, and is flown on that rate (eqs (51)–(53)).

Declaring a period of your own

In fixed altitude mode, 2 · Declare a period of your own holds the declared pair:

  • It starts on the nearest repeat.
  • Selecting a grid row copies that row’s pair into it.
  • You can type any pair, for example from an existing filing or a published constellation.

The pair is checked like a row the search found:

  • A pair with a common factor is reduced first, and the line below the fields says so. 26 orbits in 2 days becomes 13/1: the track has already repeated after 13 orbits, and the second time round adds nothing.
  • Its exact altitude is solved anywhere from 100 to 30 000 km, not only inside the search band.
  • It becomes the highlighted top row of the grid, selectable like any other. It turns red when its exact altitude lies outside the search band: the repeat exists, but not near your orbit.

The line below the fields gives the declared altitude, the drift per cycle at that altitude, and the altitude where the pair repeats by itself. It reads no altitude between 100 and 30000 km closes this pair when none does, and asks for whole numbers of at least 1 when an entry is not one.

The tolerance, keep_rnge

keep_rnge is the longitude tolerance your filing promises either side of each track. You choose it; the solver only bounds it. It is the same value as keep_rnge (deg) on the Station-keeping cases sub-tab.

  • It must be above 0 and below the selected row’s max keep_rnge, half the track spacing. At half the spacing the tolerance bands of neighbouring tracks touch.
  • The line below the field gives that bound. In fixed altitude mode it adds how many cycles the drift takes to cross keep_rnge; the Case 2 panel on the Station-keeping cases sub-tab turns that into days between a real satellite’s corrections.
  • Out of bounds, the line turns red and begins keep_rnge out of bounds. Save design on the Constellation sub-tab refuses a Case 2 or Case 3 shell whose keep_rnge is out of bounds or that has no selected row.

The ground track

Selecting a row runs one cycle of the declared orbit through the app’s orbit propagator, for one satellite without station keeping, and draws its ground track on the map. The filled dot marks the start, the ring the end. The line under How the solver works gives the angle between them: track closure after one cycle.

  • In adjusting altitude mode the cycle runs at the exact altitude for cycle@exact. Dot and ring coincide; for the rows above the closure reads 0.0000 deg.
  • In fixed altitude mode the cycle runs at your target altitude for cycle@target. The closure equals the size of drift@target, and the line says so: it is the drift the EPFD calculation flies every cycle, not an error of the map.

The map always shows the row as a Case 2 shell would file it. It does not draw the Case 3 model.

whole constellation, top right of the map, overlays one cycle of every satellite of every shell in the document, each shell at its own altitude and over its own cycle. The dot and ring are then hidden. Every plane of a shell shares its repeat, so the shell’s pattern repeats with it. The phasing between planes decides only whether the satellites share the same k track lines or run between them, and the overlay shows which.

The constellation repeat

With several station-kept shells, the constellation repeats only when every shell’s track has repeated. That period, P_repeat, is the shortest time that holds a whole number of every shell’s declared cycle.

  • It is shown under the shell list on Start here and at the top of the Constellation sub-tab, with how many of each shell’s cycles it holds.
  • The line warns when the period is 101 days or longer, and when some shells repeat and others do not. Case 2 and Case 3 shells both count as repeating.
  • Harmonize rpt_prd, on the Constellation sub-tab while the shell is Case 2 or Case 3, declares that period as rpt_prd on every shell. A whole number of a shell’s own cycles is also a valid repeat period for its track. It needs every shell to be Case 2 or Case 3 with a selected row, and names the shell that is not. Changing a shell’s pair, its selected row, its case or its altitude mode clears that shell’s harmonized period.
Tech · one period for all shells

Rec. ITU-R S.1503-4 §A2.4 asks for one repeat period appropriate for all satellites, including all sub-constellations, and §B5.1 for shells that are all repeating or all non-repeating. §D4.6 defines P_repeat, the time for every satellite to return to the same position relative to the Earth, and the run duration T_run = N_run · P_repeat. The tab computes P_repeat as the least common multiple of the shells’ declared rpt_prd in whole seconds. Within one shell nothing needs harmonizing: all its satellites share the cycle.
