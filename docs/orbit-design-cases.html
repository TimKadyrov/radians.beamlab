The Three Orbit Cases

Before a non-GSO constellation can operate, its filing goes through the EPFD calculation — and the filing must say how the system holds its path over the Earth. There are exactly three answers: the three orbit cases. This page explains what they mean, why they exist, and how the Orbit Design tab turns your orbit numbers into the fields the filing needs.

Why cases exist at all

The EPFD calculation (equivalent power flux-density — the measure of how much interference a constellation could cause to other satellite systems) works by simulation: it replays the satellites’ motion for a while and accumulates the interference reaching a victim. For the result to be fair, the calculation has to see the constellation from every angle it can actually take up — not just the slice of geometry that happened to occur during the run.

The complication is that satellite paths do not stand still. The Earth is slightly wider at the equator than pole to pole, and that extra bulge tugs on every tilted orbit, slowly turning its path westward. Left alone, the line a satellite traces over the ground drifts a little with every lap and never comes back to where it started. So the calculation needs to know: does the operator fight that drift, declare it, or let it run? That single question is the whole classification:

Technical detail

The three cases and their parameters are defined in Rec. ITU-R S.1503-4, §B3.1 (DoesRepeat with W_delta; AdminSuppliedPrecession with ORBIT_PRECESS; neither flag = free drift), and the propagation each one implies in §D6.3. The westward turning of the orbit plane is the J2 nodal regression.

The one number everything is built on

Watch a satellite cross the equator heading north, then watch its next crossing one lap later. The second crossing lands a fixed distance west of the first — partly because the Earth rotated underneath, partly because of the bulge’s slow turning of the orbit. That fixed westward step per lap is called S_pass, and all three cases are just different kinds of arithmetic on it. The Orbit Design tab computes it from three numbers you already know about your orbit: its altitude, its tilt (inclination), and how stretched it is (eccentricity).

Technical detail — how S_pass is computed
n̄  = n₀ [1 + 1.5 J₂ (Re/p)² (1 − 1.5 sin²i) √(1−e²)]
Ω̇  = −1.5 J₂ (Re/p)² n̄ cos i     ω̇ = 1.5 J₂ (Re/p)² n̄ (2 − 2.5 sin²i)
T_nodal = 360 / (ω̇ + n̄)     S_pass = (ω_e − Ω̇) · T_nodal

The J2 secular rates of Rec. S.1503-4 §D6.3 — the same ones its propagator integrates, so what the tab predicts is what the simulation does.

CASE 1Free drift — the calculation adds its own correction

The situation. The operator does nothing about the drift. The ground track slides west forever and never repeats.

The problem that creates. The EPFD calculation cannot run forever. Cut a drifting track off after any finite time and some longitudes have been visited many times while others were never visited at all — the interference result would depend on where the run happened to start and stop.

The fix. The Recommendation has the calculation itself add a tiny extra drift — an artificial precession — sized so that a chosen number of laps (NOrbits) spreads the crossings perfectly evenly around the equator and then closes. Every longitude gets sampled equally, and the run has a natural end. Important: this is a simulation device, not a property of the satellite — the filing declares nothing for it.

Technical detail — what the tab’s Case 1 panel computes
S_grid  = 360 · floor(NOrbits · S_pass / 360) / NOrbits
rate    = (S_grid − S_pass) / T_nodal   (added to the node rate)
run     = NOrbits · T_nodal

Rec. S.1503-4 §D6.3.2, Steps 8–11. NOrbits comes from the Recommendation’s run-geometry rules; in the tab it is an input.

A known quirk. Implementations that transcribe the Recommendation’s Steps 8–11 literally end up with a measured crossing spacing of 2·S_pass − S_grid — one correction step past the even grid, the opposite direction from the stated goal. The error is small (bounded by two grid cells per lap) and identical across literal implementations, which matters more for comparing results than hitting the ideal spacing. The tab prints the measured value alongside the ideal one so the difference is never a surprise.
filed: nothing — both station-keeping answers are "no"

CASE 2Station-kept — a promised, repeating track

The situation. The operator actively steers the constellation so its ground track repeats: after a fixed number of laps, every satellite retraces the same lines over the Earth, staying within a promised longitude tolerance either side of them.

What the filing must say. Two things: how long one full repeat takes (the repeat period), and how wide the tolerance is (the keep range). The EPFD calculation then simulates exactly one declared cycle and sweeps the satellite across the tolerance band, because within the promise the system may be anywhere in it.

The catch. A track only repeats by itself at special altitudes: it closes after k laps precisely when k westward steps add up to whole turns of the Earth, and at an arbitrary altitude they almost never do. But the promise is the operator’s, not the orbit’s — in practice the mission altitude is fixed, the repeat is declared at it, and the station keeping corrects the small natural drift; that is exactly what the tolerance band exists for. The tab shows both readings: what the repeat costs in correction at your altitude (the default declaration), and the nearby altitudes where the equality holds exactly and the repeat costs nothing.

Technical detail — what the tab’s grid and Case 2 panel compute
find coprime (k, m):  k · S_pass(a) = 360 · m   (solved for the altitude a near your target)
repeat period  = k · T_nodal(a)  →  the filing’s dd/hh/mm/ss fields
spacing  = 360/k     keep range < 180/k

Rec. S.1503-4 §B3.1 (DoesRepeat, W_delta) and §D6.3; the tolerance sweep is the Recommendation’s Case-2 propagation behaviour.

filed: station keeping = yes  ·  keep range (deg)  ·  repeat period (dd/hh/mm/ss)

The map at the bottom of the tab shows the promise being kept: one full cycle of the selected candidate, flown by the real propagator. The dot is where the track started, the ring where it ended — when they sit on top of each other, the track closed, and the printed closure angle is the same statement as a number.

CASE 3Declared drift — the filed rate is used as-is

The situation. Some systems neither hold a repeating track nor drift passively — they manage their orbit’s slow turn deliberately, for instance to keep a constant relationship with the Sun. For these, the operator simply declares the turning rate, and the EPFD calculation propagates the constellation with exactly the filed number instead of inventing a correction.

What the tab offers. The natural rate for an unmanaged orbit at your chosen geometry — the honest default to file when the drift is real but undirected. A system that actively manages the rate files its managed value instead.

Technical detail — the tab’s Case 3 panel
declared rate = −1.5 · n · J₂ · (Re/p)² · cos i   (deg/s)

The standard J2 nodal regression at the filed altitude, eccentricity and inclination; the case itself is Rec. S.1503-4 §B3.1 (AdminSuppliedPrecession, ORBIT_PRECESS).

filed: station keeping = no  ·  precession = yes, rate in deg/s

From your numbers to the filing — the whole chain

Everything above is one pipeline: three numbers describe the orbit, one computation gives the westward step, and the step branches into the three filings. The tab shows all three cases at once for whichever candidate you select, so they are compared on the same orbit rather than imagined separately.
